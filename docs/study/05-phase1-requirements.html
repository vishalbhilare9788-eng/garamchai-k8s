Requirements Before YAML

How an architect turns a client's wishes into numbers and decisions:
features, order lifecycle, load math, availability, RPO/RTO, data protection and access
The core idea Beginners start with YAML. Architects start with questions. Every Kubernetes object we use later should exist because a requirement needs it. In an interview, "why did you use X?" should always have an answer that starts with a requirement.

2.3 The order lifecycle

Only a PLACED order can be cancelled by the customer, rejected, or expired. Rejection reasons: CLOSED, OUT_OF_STOCK, OTHER, plus a suggestion text ("no masala chai today, try ginger chai!"). Customers pay at the counter on pickup.

4. Non-functional 1: load, step by step

Piece 1: a request

Every time a browser asks the server for something, that's 1 request, like one customer walking up to the counter and asking something.

Piece 2: "50 users online" ≠ "50 requests now"

50 customers inside a chai shop aren't all talking to the counter at the same second. Most are reading, drinking, chatting. Online is the same: concurrent means the site is open, not that everyone is clicking.

Piece 3: one user

1 user does something about once every 10 seconds
→ 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1 requests per second

Piece 4: fifty users

50 users × 0.1 = 5 requests per second   (average)

Piece 5: rush hour

5 × 4 (safety factor for the 8–10 am and 5–7 pm rush) = 20 requests per second   ← design target

Piece 6: is 20 a lot?

No. One small server answers thousands of simple requests per second. That's why the charter honestly says this app doesn't need Kubernetes. We use it to learn. In the Phase 10 load test we'll push far beyond 20 on purpose to watch HPA scale.

Where does most of the traffic come from? Not browsing: waiting. A customer watching the status page checks every 5 s for ~5 minutes, which is ~60 requests. Knowing that, you could check less often or push updates to the browser instead.

Formula (Little's law style): requests/s ≈ users ÷ seconds between clicks. 200 users → 20 req/s average → 80 at rush hour.

5. Non-functional 2: availability

Piece 1: turn the percentage into minutes

A month ≈ 30 days × 24 h = 720 hours

99.5 %  → down 0.5 %  → 720 × 0.005  = 3.6 hours per month   ← our target
99.9 %  → down 0.1 %  → 720 × 0.001  = 0.72 h ≈ 43 minutes
99.99 % → down 0.01 % → 720 × 0.0001 = 0.072 h ≈ 4.3 minutes

Piece 2: why not promise more?

We have one control plane, one Postgres and the NFS server on cka-m. If cka-m is down, running pods keep serving, but nothing can be changed, and anything using NFS stops. Promising 99.9% would be a lie; 99.5% is honest, and we'll measure it.

Piece 3: zero-downtime deploys are separate, and free

Planned updates must not take the site down. Kubernetes gives us this for free: rolling updates, readiness probes, PodDisruptionBudgets, at least 2 replicas of stateless services.

The money math, piece by piece

1 hour down at the evening rush:  50 orders × ₹100 = ₹5,000 lost
99.5 % allows 3.6 h/month; worst case all at rush hour: 3.6 × ₹5,000 = ₹18,000/month
99.99 % would need multi-site servers, a replicated database and a 24×7 on-call team:
   one engineer's salary alone is more than ₹18,000/month, before a single server
→ spending lakhs to save ₹18,000 is like a ₹10 lakh alarm for a ₹20,000 bicycle
RecommendationWhyCost

The lessonAvailability is a business decision. Put two numbers side by side: what downtime costs, and what preventing it costs. Pick the level where the second is smaller. The full 99.99% treatment (SLA/SLO/SLI, MTTR, multi-region AWS, cost) is in docs/guides/high-availability-99.99.pdf.

7. Security 1: protecting data

7.1 List the data and rank it

DataIf it leaks…
Menu, pricesNothing, it's public
OrdersA little embarrassing
Email + phonePersonal data: spam, scams, legal trouble
PasswordsVery bad: people reuse them for bank and email
Keys, DB passwordsWorst: an attacker becomes the app

7.2 Passwords: store a fingerprint, not the password

Sign-up: "chai@123" → bcrypt → "$2b$12$Kx9...f3"   ← only this is saved
Login:   "chai@123" → bcrypt → same fingerprint?   → yes = correct password
  • A hash is one-way: a stolen hash can't be turned back into the password.
  • But attackers can still guess: hash a million common passwords and compare. bcrypt defends by being slow on purpose and adding a random salt per user (same password → different hash).

7.3 A Secret isn't secret by default

  • A Kubernetes Secret is only base64-encoded: echo cGFzc3dvcmQ= | base64 -d gives password to anyone.
  • It's readable by anyone with RBAC get secrets, and by anyone with access to etcd or its backups.
  • Encryption at rest (Phase 8) encrypts Secrets inside etcd with a symmetric key (e.g. AES): one key both encrypts and decrypts. Private/public key pairs (asymmetric) are a different thing, used in TLS certificates.
#RuleLeads to
S1Passwords only as bcrypt hashesauth-service code
S2Keys and DB passwords only in Secrets; only *.example.yaml in GitSecret, .gitignore
S3Encryption at rest for Secrets in etcdEncryptionConfiguration (Phase 8)
S4One DB user per service; only auth-service reads personal dataPostgres roles (Phase 5)
S5TLS at the edge (browser → Traefik); inside, default-deny NetworkPolicyIngress/Gateway + cert-manager, NetworkPolicy

Not chosen: mTLS between services everywhere. It needs a service mesh, which is too heavy for our RAM (common in banks and large shared clusters).

8. Security 2: who may access the cluster

StepQuestionFailure
AuthenticationWho are you?401
Authorization (RBAC)What may you do?403

Humans are names in certificates (CN = name, O = group) signed by the cluster CA; there's no User object. Programs are ServiceAccount objects. Client certificates can't be revoked, so a stolen admin certificate works until it expires; that's why the CA key and admin.conf are guarded (details in study notes 1, §11).

#WhoGets
A1Vishal (admin)admin.conf: never shared, never committed
A2"intern" userRead-only in garamchai only (CSR + Role/RoleBinding, Phase 8)
A3Each serviceOwn ServiceAccount, no API permissions, automountServiceAccountToken: false
A4Backup JobSmall Role in garamchai-data only
A5SSH to nodesVishal only, key-based

Deployments: CI (GitHub Actions) only builds and pushes images; deploys run from the laptop. The cluster sits behind VMware NAT, so GitHub can't reach it, and opening it would put cluster credentials on a third-party server. The real-world alternative is GitOps: an agent inside the cluster (e.g. Argo CD) pulls from Git, so no credentials leave the cluster.

  3. From which state can an order be cancelled by the customer? What happens if staff never respond?
  4. Why did we add F11 (open/closed, stock) instead of just rejecting orders?
  5. Why is item substitution out of scope?
  6. Why does the sign-up form need an opt-in checkbox?
  7. 50 users each click once every 10 s. How many requests per second on average, and at rush hour (×4)?
  8. 200 users, same behaviour: average and rush-hour requests per second?
  9. Which user activity causes the most requests, and why?
  10. How many hours per month of downtime does 99.5 % allow? Show the math.
  11. Why is 99.9 % not an honest promise for our lab?
  12. Define RPO and RTO in one line each. What's our RPO for orders, and what does it force us to build?
  13. The site is down for 2 hours at rush hour. How much is lost (₹5,000/hour)?
  14. Why is 99.99 % a bad deal for GaramChai? Use numbers.
  15. Why store a bcrypt hash instead of the password? What do slowness and salt add?
  16. Why is a Kubernetes Secret not truly secret by default? Who can read it?
  17. Does encryption at rest use a private key? Explain.
  18. Why TLS at the edge and not mTLS everywhere for us?
  19. Why do our services get no ServiceAccount token?
  20. Why don't we let GitHub Actions deploy to the cluster? What's the GitOps alternative?

Answer key

  1. Every object should exist because a requirement needs it; that's what makes design defensible. E.g. "admins upload photos" → ReadWriteMany PVC; "nightly report" → CronJob.
  2. Customer, staff, admin. Staff accept/reject orders and move them through PREPARING → READY → COMPLETED.
  3. Only while PLACED. With no staff response within 10 min it becomes EXPIRED.
  4. Prevention beats rejection: customers shouldn't be able to place orders that will be rejected. REJECTED stays for surprises.
  5. It needs a waiting state, notifications and timeouts (a negotiation workflow) and teaches no new Kubernetes object. A suggestion in the rejection message covers most of the value.
  6. Sending offers requires consent under India's DPDP Act 2023 (and GDPR); it's off by default.
  7. 50 × 0.1 = 5 req/s average; × 4 = 20 req/s at rush hour.
  8. 200 × 0.1 = 20 req/s average; 80 at rush hour.
  9. Waiting for READY: the status page checks every 5 s for about 5 minutes (~60 requests per order).
  10. 720 h × 0.005 = 3.6 hours.
  11. Single control plane, single Postgres, NFS on cka-m: one machine failing takes parts of the app down for longer than 43 minutes.
  12. RPO: how much data you may lose (time since the last good copy). RTO: how long recovery may take. RPO 4 h → a Postgres backup CronJob every 4 hours (plus a restore drill).
  13. 2 × ₹5,000 = ₹10,000.
  14. 99.5 % worst case loses ~₹18,000/month; 99.99 % needs multi-site infrastructure and a 24×7 team costing lakhs per month. You'd spend far more than the downtime costs.
  15. A hash is one-way, so stolen hashes don't reveal passwords. Slowness makes mass guessing take years; a per-user salt makes identical passwords hash differently.
  16. It's only base64-encoded. Anyone with RBAC get secrets, or with access to etcd or its backups, can read it.
  17. No. It uses a symmetric key (e.g. AES) that both encrypts and decrypts. Private/public keys are asymmetric crypto, used for TLS.
  18. mTLS everywhere needs a service mesh, too heavy for our RAM. TLS at the edge + default-deny NetworkPolicy is proportionate.
  19. Our apps never call the Kubernetes API. Without a token, a hacked pod gives the attacker no cluster access (least privilege).
  20. GitHub can't reach a cluster behind NAT, and exposing it would put cluster credentials on a third-party server. GitOps: an in-cluster agent like Argo CD pulls changes from Git.
