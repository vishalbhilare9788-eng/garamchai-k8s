1. The example: PayKaro

We need one concrete app to hang every idea on. PayKaro (fictional) is an Indian payments app: customers pay shops by scanning a QR code, and the money moves from the customer's wallet to the merchant's.

Fact about PayKaroValue (our assumption)Why it matters
Transactions per day5 millionSizing (Part 5.5)
Busiest hour7–9 pm (shopping, dinner)Downtime at this hour hurts most
PayKaro earns per transaction₹2 (fee)Cost of downtime (Part 9)
Client's demand99.99% availabilityThe whole guide
Where it runsAWS, IndiaIndian rules on where payment data may be stored
Why a payments app? Because it makes every trade-off sharp. For a chai shop (GaramChai) an outage is annoying. For a payments app an outage means shoppers standing at a counter unable to pay, merchants losing sales, a regulator asking questions and a brand losing trust. And a lost transaction isn't lost chai, it's lost money.

2. The vocabulary

2.1 Availability

Plain meaning: the share of time the service works for its users.

Availability = time it worked ÷ total time

Example: a 30-day month has 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200 minutes.
         The app was down for 43 minutes.
         Availability = (43,200 − 43) ÷ 43,200 = 0.99900 = 99.90 %

Analogy: a shop's opening hours. If the shop promises to be open all day and is shut for 43 minutes in a month, it was "open 99.9% of the time".

2.2 The "nines": turning a percentage into minutes

Percentages hide how small the numbers get. Always convert them into minutes, which is what the business actually feels.

Allowed downtime = total time × (100 % − availability)

99.99 % over a 30-day month:
   step 1: 100 % − 99.99 % = 0.01 %  = 0.0001
   step 2: 43,200 minutes × 0.0001   = 4.32 minutes
Availability"Nines"per yearper 30-day monthper weekper day
99 %two3.65 days7.2 h1.7 h14.4 min
99.5 %two and a half1.83 days3.6 h50 min7.2 min
99.9 %three8.76 h43.2 min10.1 min1.4 min
99.95 %three and a half4.38 h21.6 min5.0 min43 s
99.99 %four52.6 min4.32 min1.0 min8.6 s
99.999 %five5.3 min26 s6 s0.9 s

Some sources use an average month of 30.44 days, so you'll also see "99% = 7.3 h per month". Both are fine; say which one you used.

The one fact that drives the whole design At 99.99% you may be down for 4 minutes a month. A human paged at 3 am needs longer than that just to wake up, read the alert and log in. So at four nines, recovery must be automatic. Humans find the root cause afterwards; machines restore service.

2.3 SLI, SLO, SLA: three words people mix up

Think of a pizza shop that promises "delivered in 30 minutes or it's free".

TermPlain meaningPizza analogyPayKaro example
SLI
Service Level Indicator		A measurement: a number you compute from real data"How many minutes did each delivery actually take?"% of payment requests that succeeded within 1 second
SLO
Service Level Objective		An internal target for that measurement"We aim for 99% of deliveries under 25 min"99.99% of payments succeed within 1 s, over 30 days
SLA
Service Level Agreement		A contract with a customer, with a penalty if missed"Over 30 min: the pizza is free"To merchants: 99.95% per month, or a fee refund

How they fit together

SLI  = what you MEASURE     (the speedometer)
SLO  = what you AIM for     (your own speed rule: "I drive at 90")
SLA  = what you PROMISE     (the legal limit: 100, with a fine)

Rule: SLA is looser than SLO.  SLO 99.99 %  →  SLA 99.95 %
      The gap is your safety margin: you notice and fix things
      BEFORE you owe anybody money.

A good SLI is measured the way the user feels it

SLI (payments) = good payment requests ÷ all valid payment requests

"good"  = answered without a server error (5xx) AND in under 1 second
"valid" = excludes requests the user got wrong (bad PIN, bad QR) → those are 4xx

A payment that "succeeds" after 40 seconds has failed for the shopper at the counter, which is why slow counts as bad.

Trap: what cloud SLAs really promise AWS SLAs don't guarantee uptime. They define service credits: if a service falls below a threshold, you get a partial refund of that service's bill. Example: Aurora Multi-AZ gives a 10% credit below 99.99%. If PayKaro loses ₹50 lakh in an outage, AWS refunds a slice of the database bill. Your SLA to your customers is your problem, not AWS's. An SLA is a financial instrument, not an engineering guarantee.

2.4 Error budget: turning the SLO into a spending allowance

Plain meaning: if the target is 99.99%, then 0.01% of failure is allowed. That allowance is a budget you can "spend".

Time-based:     30 days × 0.01 %      = 4.32 minutes of downtime per month
Request-based:  200 M payments × 0.01 % = 20,000 failed payments per month

PayKaro: 5 M/day × 30 = 150 M/month  →  150 M × 0.0001 = 15,000 failures allowed

What it's for: it ends the endless fight between "ship features fast" and "don't break anything".

  • Budget left → ship features, run experiments, take risks.
  • Budget spent → freeze risky releases; the team works on reliability until the budget recovers.

Burn rate: how fast you're spending it

Burn rate 1  = spending exactly on plan (budget lasts the full 30 days)
Burn rate 10 = spending 10× too fast   (whole budget gone in 30 ÷ 10 = 3 days)

Alert rule used by many SRE teams (multi-window burn rate):
  page a human if burn rate > 14.4 over the last 1 hour
  (14.4 × 1 h = 2 % of a 30-day budget gone in one hour)
Interview line "SLIs are measurements from the user's point of view, SLOs are our internal targets, SLAs are contracts with penalties and are always looser than the SLO. The gap between 100% and the SLO is the error budget; when it's spent we slow releases and fix reliability."

Self-check

  1. How many minutes per 30-day month does 99.95% allow? (Answer: 43,200 × 0.0005 = 21.6.)
  2. Why should an SLA be looser than the SLO?
  3. A payment that succeeds after 30 seconds: good or bad for the SLI? Why?

3. The failure clock: MTBF, MTTD, MTTA, MTTR

Every outage follows the same timeline. Each letter-soup term is just one stretch of that timeline.

TermStands forPlain meaningHow to make it better
MTBFMean Time Between FailuresHow long things usually work before the next failure. Bigger is better.Redundancy, testing, safer deploys, fewer risky changes
MTTDMean Time To DetectHow long until anyone (or anything) noticesGood SLIs, synthetic checks every minute, burn-rate alerts
MTTAMean Time To AcknowledgeHow long until a human says "I'm on it"On-call rotation, clear paging, no alert noise
MTTRMean Time To Recover (also: Repair / Resolve)From failure start to service working again. Smaller is better.Automatic failover, one-click rollback, runbooks

"MTTR" is ambiguous: Recover, Repair, Respond and Resolve are all used. In an interview, say which one you mean. For availability, the one that matters is time to recover service.

3.1 The formula that connects them

Availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)

Plain words: of every "cycle" (working time + broken time), which share was working?

3.2 Worked example: how fast must PayKaro recover?

Suppose PayKaro has 1 incident per month.
   MTBF ≈ 30 days = 43,200 minutes
   Target availability = 99.99 %

   0.9999 = 43,200 ÷ (43,200 + MTTR)
   43,200 + MTTR = 43,200 ÷ 0.9999 = 43,204.32
   MTTR = 4.32 minutes            ← the whole incident, detect-to-recovered
Now the human reality of ONE incident:
   detect (alert needs a few minutes of bad data)   2 min
   page reaches a sleepy engineer, acknowledged      5 min
   open laptop, VPN, dashboards, understand          10 min
   decide and act (failover / rollback)              10 min
   ------------------------------------------------------
   total                                              27 min   ≈ 6× over budget
Conclusion With humans in the loop, one incident a month is enough to miss 99.99%. There are only two ways out, and a 99.99% design uses both:
  1. Recover automatically in seconds (health checks + automatic failover + automatic rollback), so MTTR is small.
  2. Fail less often (redundancy, safer deploys), so MTBF is big.

Self-check

  1. If PayKaro had 4 incidents a month, how long could each one last at 99.99%? (Answer: 4.32 ÷ 4 ≈ 65 seconds.)
  2. Which of MTTD / MTTA / MTTR does automatic failover shrink?

4. Losing data vs. losing time: RPO and RTO

Availability asks "is it up?". Two more questions matter in a disaster: "how much data did we lose?" and "how long until we're back?"

TermQuestion it answersAnalogy: writing a documentPayKaro target
RPO
Recovery Point Objective		How much recent data may we lose? (measured as time)You last pressed "Save" 10 minutes ago, then the laptop died: RPO = 10 minutes of typing lost≈ 0 for the ledger (a lost payment is lost money); minutes for analytics
RTO
Recovery Time Objective		How long may recovery take?How long until you're typing again on another laptop< 5 minutes for payments

4.1 What decides RPO: how the copy is made

Copy methodHow it worksRPOPrice
Nightly backupCopy once a dayup to 24 hcheap
Point-in-time recoveryBackup + continuous transaction logabout 5 mincheap
Asynchronous replicationPrimary says "saved" first, sends the copy a moment laterseconds (whatever was in flight)medium; no slowdown
Synchronous replicationPrimary says "saved" only after the copy confirms0every write waits for the other side, so it's slower the farther away the copy is
The physics you can't negotiate with Synchronous copies inside one AWS region (between Availability Zones, a few km apart) cost about a millisecond per write, which is fine. Synchronous copies between regions hundreds of km apart add tens of milliseconds to every write. That's why the common pattern is synchronous inside a region, asynchronous between regions, and you design around the few seconds of cross-region RPO (Part 7.4).

4.2 Related terms you'll hear

  • MTD / MTPD (Maximum Tolerable Downtime / Period of Disruption): the point where the business suffers serious damage. RTO must be comfortably below it.
  • Durability vs. availability: durability = "will my data still exist?"; availability = "can I reach it right now?". Amazon S3 Standard is designed for 99.999999999% (eleven nines) durability, but its availability is far lower (it's designed for 99.99%, and the SLA credit threshold is 99.9%). A service can be down while your data is perfectly safe.
  • BCP vs. DR: the Business Continuity Plan covers the whole business (people, offices, suppliers). Disaster Recovery is the IT part of it.
Interview line "RPO is how much data we can afford to lose, looking back from the disaster; RTO is how long we can afford to be down, looking forward. RPO is decided by how we copy data: sync gives zero, async gives seconds, backups give hours. RTO is decided by how automated the recovery is."

5. Availability math: chains and backups

5.1 Serial (a chain): every link must work

Analogy: Christmas lights wired in a chain. One bulb fails, the whole string goes dark. A request that passes through a load balancer, then the app, then the database needs all three.

Rule: multiply the availabilities.

Load balancer 99.99 %  ×  App 99.99 %  ×  Database 99.99 %
   = 0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.9999
   = 0.9997
   = 99.97 %          ← LOWER than every single part
Key insight A chain is always weaker than its weakest link. Every extra hop (another microservice, another external API) pulls the total down. Ten services at 99.9% in a chain give 0.99910 ≈ 99.0%.

5.2 Parallel (backups): only one needs to work

Analogy: two lamps in a room. The room is dark only if both fail at the same time.

Rule: availability = 1 − (chance that ALL copies are down)

Two copies, each 99 % (each down 1 % of the time):
   chance both down = 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001
   availability     = 1 − 0.0001  = 0.9999 = 99.99 %

Two copies, each 99.9 %:
   both down = 0.001 × 0.001 = 0.000001  →  99.9999 %

This is the magic of redundancy: two "ordinary" things in parallel beat one excellent thing.

But: the two hidden assumptions
  1. Independence. The math assumes the copies fail independently. Two servers in the same rack, on the same power feed, running the same buggy release, fail together. That's why copies go in different Availability Zones (separate power, cooling, network) and different regions, and why a bad deploy is rolled out to one copy at a time.
  2. Instant failover. The math assumes switching to the backup takes zero time. Real failover takes seconds to minutes, and that switching time counts as downtime. In practice MTTR of the failover, not the parallel formula, sets your real availability.

5.3 Putting both together (the method)

  1. Draw the request path from the user to the data.
  2. Components one after another → multiply.
  3. Components that back each other up → 1 − (product of their unavailabilities).
  4. Subtract the realistic failover time.
  5. Compare with the target.

5.4 Worked example: PayKaro in ONE AWS region

Using AWS's published SLA credit thresholds (checked September 2026) as conservative numbers:

Component (Multi-AZ)SLA threshold
Application Load Balancer (Multi-AZ)99.99 %
EC2 instances across ≥ 2 AZs (EKS worker nodes; Region-Level SLA)99.99 %
Aurora PostgreSQL, Multi-AZ cluster99.99 %
One region = 0.9999 × 0.9999 × 0.9999 = 0.9997 = 99.97 %
           = 43,200 × 0.0003 ≈ 13 minutes down per month (on paper)
Target 99.99 % = 4.3 minutes            →  ONE REGION DOESN'T MEET IT ON PAPER
Two honest caveats
  • SLA numbers are pessimistic. They're credit thresholds, not measured availability; real services usually do better. Composing SLAs gives a conservative floor, which is exactly what you want when you're signing a contract.
  • The Kubernetes control plane isn't in the request path. EKS's standard control plane SLA is 99.95%, but if the API server is down, the pods keep serving traffic; you just can't deploy or scale for a while. So it doesn't go into the chain for "can a customer pay?". It does matter for your MTTR, because you can't roll back without it. (EKS also offers a Provisioned Control Plane tier with a 99.99% SLA.)

5.5 Worked example: PayKaro in TWO regions

Each region: 99.97 %  →  unavailable 0.0003 of the time

Both regions down at once (if independent):
   0.0003 × 0.0003 = 0.00000009   →  availability 99.99999 %   (on paper)

Reality check with failover time:
   Assume a serious regional problem once a year, lasting 2 hours.
   Single region:  120 min down  >  52.6 min yearly budget   →  SLO BROKEN for the whole year
   Two regions, automatic failover in 5 min:
                    5 min down    ≈ 10 % of the 52.6 min budget  →  SLO survives
Conclusion for PayKaro 99.99% needs at least two regions with automatic, practised failover. Within each region, everything runs across three Availability Zones. That's the design in Part 7.

5.6 Sizing: how many transactions per second?

5,000,000 transactions ÷ 86,400 seconds per day = 58 per second (average)
Evening peak: assume 4 × average                = ~230 per second
Design target with headroom                     = 300 per second

This is Little's-law style thinking again: average rate first, then a peak factor, then headroom.

Self-check

  1. Four services in a chain, each 99.9%. Total? (0.9994 ≈ 99.6%)
  2. Two independent copies, each 99.5%. Total? (1 − 0.005 × 0.005 = 99.9975%)
  3. Why does "two copies in the same rack" break the parallel formula?

6. The questions an architect asks before saying "yes, 99.99%"

The best first answer to "we need 99.99%" isn't "yes". It's a set of questions, because each extra nine costs roughly 10× more.

6.1 Business questions (always first)

QuestionWhy it mattersPayKaro answer (example)
What does 1 hour of downtime cost?Sets how much reliability is worth paying for≈ ₹16.6 lakh at peak, plus penalties and reputation (Part 9)
Which user journeys need 99.99%?Not everything needs the same level"Pay" and "check balance": 99.99%. Statements, offers, profile: 99.9%

6.2 Technical questions

  • Single points of failure: is there anything with only one copy? (one database writer, one NAT gateway, one DNS provider, one person who knows the system)
  • Dependencies: list every external service in the payment path (bank/UPI switch, SMS OTP, fraud check). What's their SLA? What does PayKaro do when one is down: fail, queue, or degrade?
  • Data consistency: can two regions accept writes for the same account? (For a ledger: usually no, because two regions both debiting the same wallet is how money gets double-spent.)
  • Failure detection: how fast do we know, and who decides to fail over: a machine or a person?
  • Changes: most outages are caused by our own deployments and configuration changes. How are they rolled out and rolled back?
  • Capacity: if one AZ (or one region) disappears, can the rest carry 100% of the load immediately?

6.3 People and process questions

  • Is there a 24×7 on-call rotation? (Needs about 5–8 people so nobody is on call every week.)
  • Are there runbooks, and have failovers been practised (game days)?
  • Is there a blameless post-incident review after every outage?
  • Who owns the error budget decision to freeze releases?

7. The design: PayKaro on AWS, layer by layer

7.1 Where the data lives (regions)

Two Indian regions: Mumbai (ap-south-1) as primary and Hyderabad (ap-south-2) as the second region. Keeping both in India matters because the Reserve Bank of India's 2018 directive requires payment-system data to be stored in India; always confirm the current rules with your compliance team.

7.2 Front door: DNS and edge

  • Route 53 with health checks and failover routing: if Mumbai's health check fails, DNS sends users to Hyderabad. Keep DNS TTLs short (for example 60 s) so clients follow quickly. (Route 53's SLA credit threshold is 100%: it's one of the most available services AWS has.)
  • Route 53 Application Recovery Controller (ARC): routing controls you flip deliberately in a regional event, so failover is one reliable action instead of a guess.
  • CloudFront + AWS WAF + Shield: absorbs traffic spikes and attacks before they reach the app.

7.3 Compute: EKS across three Availability Zones

  • Every service runs several replicas spread across 3 AZs (Kubernetes topologySpreadConstraints).
  • Static stability: if one AZ disappears, the other two must carry 100% of the load without waiting for autoscaling. So each AZ runs at most about two-thirds full. That spare capacity is the price of not depending on "scale up during the emergency".
  • Readiness probes take a sick pod out of the load balancer in seconds; liveness probes restart hung ones; PodDisruptionBudgets stop maintenance from taking too many replicas down at once.
  • HPA (pods) and Karpenter / Cluster Autoscaler (nodes) for normal growth.

7.4 Data: the hardest part

  • Aurora PostgreSQL in Mumbai: storage keeps 6 copies across 3 AZs, and a write counts only when 4 of the 6 confirm. Losing a whole AZ loses no committed data (RPO 0 inside the region), and a reader is promoted to writer automatically.
  • Aurora Global Database copies to Hyderabad asynchronously. AWS states typical cross-region lag under 1 second, an RPO of about 1 second, and promotion of the secondary region in under 1 minute.
  • One writer region at a time for the ledger. Two regions writing the same wallet would risk double-spending, so the design is "active-active for reads, active-passive for writes".
  • The ~1 second gap on regional failover: a few in-flight payments may not have reached Hyderabad. The mitigations:
    • Idempotency keys: every payment request carries a unique ID; retrying the same ID never charges twice.
    • Reconciliation: after failover, compare with the bank/UPI switch's records and repair the few affected transactions.
    • Outbox pattern: the ledger write and the "send notification" event are saved in one transaction, so events are never lost or invented.
  • Backups still matter: replication faithfully copies mistakes too (a bad migration, a deleted table, ransomware). Use point-in-time recovery plus AWS Backup copies to another region and another account, with Vault Lock so nobody, including an attacker with admin rights, can delete them.

7.6 Changes: where most outages come from

  • Canary releases: send 1% → 10% → 50% → 100% of traffic to the new version, watching the SLIs at each step; roll back automatically if error rate or latency rises.
  • Deploy one region at a time (and one cell at a time), never everywhere at once.
  • Feature flags: turn a feature off in seconds without a deploy.
  • Database migrations in backward-compatible steps (expand → migrate → contract), so the old and new versions can run side by side.

7.7 Seeing problems: observability

  • The four golden signals per service: latency, traffic, errors, saturation.
  • Synthetic checks (for example CloudWatch Synthetics) make a test payment path every minute from outside, so you learn about problems before customers do.
  • SLO burn-rate alerts (Part 2.4) instead of hundreds of noisy CPU alerts.

7.8 Practice: failures you rehearse don't surprise you

  • Game days: planned regional failover drills (quarterly), with the real on-call team.
  • Chaos engineering with AWS Fault Injection Service: kill pods, stop an AZ's instances, add latency, and check that the SLO holds.
  • Restore drills: prove backups restore, just like our etcd drill in the GaramChai lab.

8. Disaster-recovery strategies and which one fits

AWS describes four classic strategies. Think of them as how ready your second site is.

Which fits PayKaro? 99.99% (4.3 min/month) rules out backup & restore and pilot light: their RTO is longer than the whole monthly budget. The realistic choice is a hot/warm standby that's already running and already scaled (the Part 7 design), or full active/active for stateless and read traffic with a single write region for the ledger. RTO target: a few minutes, automated, practised.

9.3 The business case: is it worth it for PayKaro?

Cost of 1 hour down at the evening peak:
   230 payments/s × 3,600 s = 828,000 payments
   828,000 × ₹2 fee        = ₹16.6 lakh lost fees per hour  (≈ $20,000)
   + merchant SLA refunds, regulator scrutiny, customers switching apps (hard to price, often bigger)

Worst case (all downtime at peak):
   99.9 %  → 43 min/month × ₹16.6 lakh/60 ≈ ₹11.9 lakh/month
   99.99 % → 4.3 min/month               ≈ ₹1.2 lakh/month
   Difference                              ≈ ₹10.7 lakh/month (≈ $13,000) of direct fees

Extra cost of 99.99 %: ≈ $2,600 infra + ~$33,000 people/support ≈ $35,000/month
Reading the result like an architect On direct fees alone, 99.99% doesn't pay for itself. What tips the decision for a payments company is what the spreadsheet can't easily hold: trust (a customer who can't pay twice may switch apps for good), merchant contracts, and regulatory expectations. So for PayKaro the answer is "yes, for the Pay and Balance journeys", and deliberately not for statements, offers and analytics, which stay at 99.9%. For GaramChai the same math says 99.5% is plenty: an hour down costs ≈ ₹5,000.

RPO vs RTO?RPO = data you can lose (look back from the disaster); RTO = time to recover (look forward). RPO comes from the replication method; RTO from automation.
How do you calculate the availability of a system?Serial parts multiply; redundant parts: 1 − product of unavailabilities; then subtract failover time and check the independence assumption.
Can one AWS region give 99.99%?On SLA math, three 99.99% services in a chain give about 99.97%. Plus a regional event can exceed the yearly budget alone. For a hard 99.99% commitment: two regions with automatic failover.
Sync vs async replication?Sync: RPO 0 but every write waits, fine within a region. Async: no write penalty but seconds of RPO, used across regions. Design around the gap with idempotency and reconciliation.
Why not active/active writes for a ledger?Two regions accepting writes for the same account risk conflicts and double-spend; one writer region with fast promotion is safer.
What is static stability?Pre-provisioned capacity so that losing an AZ or region needs no scaling action during the emergency.
Replication exists, so why keep backups?Replication copies mistakes and attacks instantly. Backups (PITR, cross-account, immutable) protect against logical corruption and ransomware.
How do AWS SLAs work?They're service-credit thresholds, not guarantees; a credit is a small refund, and it doesn't cover your losses.
What causes most outages?Changes: deployments and configuration. Hence canaries, automatic rollback, one region/cell at a time, feature flags.
How do you know DR works?Practised game days and restore drills, measured against the RTO/RPO; chaos experiments with AWS FIS.
Where does the money go at 99.99%?Infra roughly doubles, but people (24×7 on-call), support and process dominate.
